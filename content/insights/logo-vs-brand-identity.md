"I just need a logo" is one of the most common ways a branding conversation starts. It is a reasonable place to begin. But a few months later, the same business is often asking different questions: which colour is ours? Which font goes on the invoice? Why does our Instagram look nothing like our website?

Those are not logo questions. They are identity questions. Understanding the difference helps you spend your budget on what your business actually needs.

## A logo is a mark

A logo is the symbol or wordmark that signs your business. It appears on your storefront, your profile picture, your packaging, and the corner of your documents.

A good logo is simple, distinctive, and legible at any size. But on its own, it can only do one job: identify you. It cannot tell someone how your proposal should look, what your social posts should feel like, or how your website should speak.

## A brand identity is a system

A brand identity is everything that makes your business recognisable *before* someone reads your name. It usually includes:

- **A logo suite** — a primary logo, plus alternate and icon versions for small or awkward spaces.
- **A colour palette** — defined colours with exact values, so your orange is the same orange everywhere.
- **Typography** — the typefaces you use for headlines and body text, and how they work together.
- **Graphic elements** — shapes, patterns, or layout habits that make a design feel like yours.
- **Brand guidelines** — a document that shows how to use all of the above, with examples.
- **Applications** — the identity applied to real things: business cards, letterheads, invoices, social templates.

The logo is one part of that system. The system is what creates consistency.

## Why consistency is the real product

People rarely decide to trust a business because of a logo. They decide because everything they see feels like it comes from the same, well-run place: the website, the quote, the business card, the delivery note.

Consistency signals care. And inconsistency—five different greens, three fonts, a stretched logo on an invoice—signals the opposite, even when the work itself is excellent.

A logo alone leaves those decisions open, to be made again each time someone creates something. An identity makes them once.

> A logo identifies you. A brand identity makes you recognisable.

## What do you need right now?

It depends on where the business is.

**If you are just starting out**, you may not need the full system yet. A focused package—one logo direction, an alternate mark, a colour palette, typography, and a short brand guide—gives you a professional start without overbuilding. That is the thinking behind our [Brand Essentials package](/pricing/).

**If you are growing**, the gaps start to show. More people are creating materials, and you are present in more places. This is when a full logo suite, brand guidelines, social templates, and stationery pay for themselves.

**If you are launching something new or repositioning**, start earlier—with [brand strategy](/services/brand-strategy/). Positioning and messaging give the design a clear reason behind it.

## Questions to ask before you commission either

Whoever you work with, these questions will help you compare like with like:

1. Which logo versions will I receive, and in which file formats?
2. Are colours and fonts defined, with exact values and names?
3. Will I get guidelines that someone else could follow?
4. Which real applications are included—business cards, letterhead, social templates?
5. How many revision rounds are included, and what happens after that?

Clear answers to those five questions tell you whether you are buying a mark or a system.

## The short version

A logo is where a brand begins, not where it ends. If your business only needs to be identified, a logo may be enough for now. If it needs to be recognised, trusted, and consistent as it grows, you need an identity.
